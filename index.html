4.1Cell theory, cell size, and microscopes

Why cells exist, why they stay small, and how we see them.

Discovery and the cell theory

Robert Hooke first described cells in 1665. Mathias Schleiden (1838) and Theodor Schwann (1839) proposed the cell theory. In its modern form:

  1. All organisms are composed of one or more cells.
  2. Cells are the smallest living things.
  3. Cells arise only from pre-existing cells.
  4. All cells today represent a continuous line of descent from the first living cells.

Why cell size is limited

Most cells are small because they rely on diffusion to move substances in and out. The rate of diffusion depends on surface area available, temperature, concentration gradient, and distance.

As a cell grows, its volume increases faster than its surface area. If the radius increases 10×, surface area increases 100× but volume increases 1000×, so the surface-to-volume ratio falls. The surface must be large enough to meet the metabolic needs of the volume. An organism made of many small cells therefore has an advantage over one made of fewer, larger cells. Some cells get around the limit by being long and skinny, like neurons.

Microscopes

Most cells are less than 50 μm across, too small to see. Resolution is the minimum distance two points can be apart and still be distinguished as two separate points. The naked eye resolves objects about 100 μm apart.

Light microscopes

  • Magnifying lenses with visible light
  • Resolve structures 200 nm apart
  • Resolution is limited by light itself

Electron microscopes

  • Beam of electrons
  • Resolve structures 0.2 nm apart
  • TEM transmits electrons through the material
  • SEM beams electrons onto the surface

The test bank also expects you to match instruments to experiments:

  • Fluorescence microscope: detects fluorescent labels, one plane of focus.
  • Confocal microscope: detects fluorescence and compiles multiple planes into a 3D image (Q40).
  • Scanning EM: 3D image of a surface only, no fluorescence.
  • Immunohistochemistry: antibodies chemically bonded to a stain reveal where a protein is in fixed tissue; a colored (non-fluorescent) stain is viewed with bright-field light microscopy (Q47).

The size-scale and microscope-picker tools are in the Tools and tables tab.

What every cell has

All cells share four features: a nucleoid or nucleus where DNA is located; cytoplasm, a semifluid matrix of organelles and cytosol; ribosomes, which synthesize proteins; and a plasma membrane, a phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins. That is why ribosomes, not a nucleus, mitochondria, or a cell wall, are required in any "minimal" cell (Q44).

Test bank trap. The Q35 mite is 0.05 mm = 50 μm. That is below the naked-eye limit (100 μm) but 250× larger than the light-microscope limit (0.2 μm). A compound light microscope is enough, and EM is unnecessary.

4.2Prokaryotic cells

The simplest organisms, in two domains: Archaea and Bacteria.

Organization

  • No membrane-bound nucleus. DNA sits in a central region called the nucleoid, which has no membrane (Q18).
  • The bacterial chromosome is a single circular DNA molecule without histone proteins (Q10).
  • Ribosomes are present, but there are no membrane-bound organelles.
  • The plasma membrane takes over some jobs organelles do in eukaryotes; for example, photosynthetic pigments in bacteria are in the plasma membrane (Q16, Q19).

Cell wall

Most bacteria are encased in a strong wall of peptidoglycan: a carbohydrate matrix cross-linked by short chains of amino acids (Q14). The wall protects the cell, maintains its shape, and prevents excessive uptake or loss of water. Walls of plants, fungi, and most protists are different. Archaea lack peptidoglycan, and archaeal membranes contain special lipids, which is what lets Sulfolobus thrive near 80 °C (Q41).

Pili and flagella

Pili are hairlike growths involved in exchange of genetic material, attachment of pathogens to host tissue, and occasionally motility. Flagella (one or more) are the structures for locomotion (Q15). Motor proteins anchored in the plasma membrane and powered by a proton gradient turn the whole flagellum; two rings sit in the cell wall. The rotation sends a spiral wave down the flagellum that pushes the cell forward.

Clinical link. Vibrio cholerae, which causes cholera, swims with a bacterial flagellum. A bacterium's susceptibility to antibiotics often depends on its cell wall structure, and many antibiotics kill bacteria by blocking prokaryotic ribosomes, e.g. streptomycin acting on the 30S subunit (Q4, Q52).

4.3Eukaryotic cells: the nucleus and ribosomes

The hallmark of eukaryotes is compartmentalization.

Eukaryotic cells have a membrane-bound nucleus, membrane-bound organelles plus an endomembrane system, and a cytoskeleton. Compartmentalization lets regions specialize, which is the test bank's explanation for why eukaryotic cells are larger than prokaryotic cells (Q62).

Shared by animal and plant cells

  • Plasma membrane
  • Most of the same organelles

Different in plant cells

  • Cell wall outside the plasma membrane
  • Chloroplasts
  • Central and other specialized vacuoles
  • Plants usually lack centrioles

Plants, fungi, and bacteria all have cell walls, but made of different materials (Q39).

Nucleus

  • Repository of genetic information; most eukaryotic cells have a single nucleus.
  • Nuclear envelope: two phospholipid bilayers.
  • Nuclear pores have 8-fold symmetry (central hub, cytoplasmic ring with fibers, nuclear ring with a basket) and control movement in and out. RNA moves out, proteins move in and out, and DNA never leaves (Q25).
  • Nuclear lamina: a dense network of intermediate filaments supporting the envelope.
  • Nucleolus: where ribosomal RNA is synthesized and ribosome subunits are assembled (Q26).
  • DNA is divided into multiple linear chromosomes. Chromatin is chromosomes plus protein. Chromosomes condense into structures visible by light microscopy usually only during cell division (Q27).

Pores are visible by freeze-fracture electron microscopy of the envelope and by TEM, where the dark material inside a pore is protein that controls access.

Ribosomes

  • The cell's protein-synthesis machinery, found in all three domains.
  • A complex of ribosomal RNA and protein (Q21), in a large and a small subunit.
  • Subunits are made in the nucleolus, exit through nuclear pores, and assemble in the cytoplasm to translate mRNA.
  • Protein synthesis also requires mRNA and tRNA, so all three RNA types come together during translation (Q48).
  • Ribosomes may be free in the cytoplasm or bound to internal membranes (rough ER).
Test bank trap. The nucleolus is inside the nucleus and has no membrane. "Nucleoid" is the prokaryotic DNA region. Q2 and Q13 both hinge on nucleus versus nucleolus versus nucleoid.

4.4The endomembrane system

A series of membranes that divides the cytoplasm into compartments. It is one of the fundamental differences between eukaryotes and prokaryotes.

Endoplasmic reticulum

Rough ER

  • Mostly flattened sacs
  • Ribosomes on the cytoplasmic face extrude new proteins into the lumen
  • Makes proteins to be secreted, sent to lysosomes, or placed in the plasma membrane

Smooth ER

  • Tubelike, connected to RER
  • Relatively few bound ribosomes
  • Synthesis (e.g. lipids), Ca²⁺ storage, detoxification

The ratio of RER to SER depends on the cell's function. Tissue with very rough ER is exporting proteins (Q36).

Golgi apparatus

Flattened stacks of membranes (Golgi bodies) that collect, modify, package, and distribute molecules made in one place and used elsewhere, inside or outside the cell. Material arrives in transport vesicles at the cis face and leaves in transport or secretory vesicles from the trans face. Plant cells have more Golgi membrane than animal cells because their Golgi also produces cell wall material (Q54).

The protein pathway

  1. Proteins made by ribosomes on the RER enter the ER; transport vesicles bud off and travel to the cis face of the Golgi.
  2. In the Golgi, proteins are modified and packaged into vesicles that bud off the trans face.
  3. Those vesicles carry proteins to other locations in the cell (e.g. lysosomes) or fuse with the plasma membrane and release their contents outside the cell.

Lysosomes

Membrane-bounded digestive vesicles that arise from the Golgi. Their hydrolytic enzymes break down macromolecules. Lysosomes fuse with their target to recycle old organelles or digest cells and foreign matter taken in by phagocytosis. Lysosomal enzymes are made on the rough ER and pass through the Golgi, so a lysosomal enzyme also shows up in the ER (Q37).

Microbodies and peroxisomes

Microbodies are enzyme-bearing, membrane-enclosed vesicles. Peroxisomes are microbodies (Q29) with enzymes for oxidation of fatty acids. They are named for the hydrogen peroxide produced as a by-product, which catalase breaks down into water and oxygen (Q9). Some contain a large protein crystal.

Vacuoles

Membrane-bound structures, typically in plants, with functions that depend on the cell: the plant central vacuole, storage vacuoles, and the contractile vacuole of some fungi and protists. The central vacuole stores dissolved substances and can expand to increase the cell's tonicity. When a plant is short of water the vacuole shrinks and the plant wilts (Q64).

Clinical links from the test bank. Adrenoleukodystrophy (ALD): long fatty acid chains build up in the CNS because a peroxisomal enzyme fails (Q28). Pompe disease (GSD II): glycogen accumulates because a lysosomal enzyme is defective (Q55). Alcohol and the liver: alcohol impairs synthesis of protein-degrading enzymes, which happens on the rough ER (Q42).
Test bank trap. Read the verb. "Synthesis of degradative enzymes" points to the rough ER; "where degradation happens" points to the lysosome (Q42). An enzyme defect causes its substrate to accumulate (Q28, Q55).

4.5Mitochondria and chloroplasts

The cell's energy converters, and both carry their own DNA.

Mitochondria

  • In all types of eukaryotic cells
  • Outer membrane, intermembrane space, inner membrane folded into cristae, and matrix
  • Proteins for oxidative metabolism sit on and in the inner membrane
  • Oxygen-requiring energy release and ATP generation (Q23, Q31)
  • Have their own DNA

Chloroplasts

  • In plants and some other eukaryotes
  • Two surrounding membranes
  • Chlorophyll for photosynthesis (Q33)
  • Thylakoids: membranous sacs inside the inner membrane; grana are stacks of thylakoids
  • CO₂ is consumed in the stroma (Q56)
  • Have their own DNA

Endosymbiosis

The endosymbiosis theory proposes that these organelles began as free-living prokaryotes that were engulfed by another cell, not digested, and became part of it (Q5). Mitochondria descend from bacteria with oxidative metabolism, and chloroplasts from photosynthetic bacteria (Q59). Both still resemble prokaryotes, for example in having their own DNA (Q30).

The nature of the engulfing cell is unknown. Two possibilities from your slides:

  1. The engulfing cell was an archaean that gave rise to the nuclear genome and cytoplasm.
  2. The engulfing cell was a bacterium carrying a nucleus derived from an archaean, produced by fusion or by the bacterium engulfing the archaean.
Clinical link. Mitochondrial disorders can come from mutations in mitochondrial DNA or nuclear DNA; there is no separate "cytoplasmic DNA" (Q58). Oxygen crosses the outer mitochondrial membrane easily (Q50).
Test bank trap. Count membranes from where the molecule is, not from outside. Water already through the chloroplast's outer membrane needs just 1 more to reach the stroma (Q60). ATP leaving the matrix crosses 2 (Q61).

4.6The cytoskeleton

A dynamic network of protein fibers in all eukaryotic cells that supports cell shape and keeps organelles in place.

The cytoskeleton is constantly forming and disassembling.

  • Microfilaments (actin filaments): two protein chains loosely twined together. They drive contraction, crawling, and "pinching".
  • Microtubules: the largest cytoskeletal elements, built from dimers of α- and β-tubulin. They move the cell and move materials within it.
  • Intermediate filaments: between actin filaments and microtubules in size, and very stable, usually not broken down. The nuclear lamina is made of them.

Centrosomes and centrioles

The centrosome is the region around the centrioles in almost all animal cells. Its pericentriolar material contains ring-shaped tubulin structures that nucleate microtubule assembly, so centrosomes are microtubule-organizing centers. Centrioles are barrel-shaped, made of nine triplets of microtubules, and occur in pairs at right angles near the nuclear membrane. Animal cells and most protists have them; plants and fungi usually lack them. Blocking centrioles with centrinone stops cell division (Q53).

Molecular motors

Vesicles are carried along microtubules by motor proteins that use ATP to generate force. Connector molecules such as the dynactin complex attach vesicles to motors. Dynein moves cargo toward the cell center (Q45); kinesin moves it toward the cell periphery.

4.7Cell movement and extracellular structures

Actin and microtubules move cells; walls and matrix surround them.

How cells move

Movement of actin filaments, microtubules, or both lets cells move. Some cells crawl using actin microfilaments; this is the movement behind the spread of cancer cells, wound healing, and blood clotting (Q34).

Eukaryotic flagella and cilia share a 9 + 2 arrangement: nine microtubule pairs around two central microtubules (Q32). Radial spokes connect the central pair to the outer pairs, which carry dynein arms. A flagellum grows directly from a basal body, which has nine microtubule triplets, like a centriole. Cilia are shorter and more numerous than flagella.

Eukaryotic cell walls

Plants, fungi, and some protists have walls that differ chemically and structurally from prokaryotic walls. Plant and protist walls are cellulose; fungal walls are chitin. Plant cell walls are thick, strong, and rigid: a primary wall is laid down when the cell is young, and a thicker secondary wall may be added once the cell is fully grown.

Extracellular matrix (ECM)

Animal cells lack walls. Instead they secrete a mixture of glycoproteins, where collagen may be abundant, forming a protective layer that gives support, strength, and resilience. Integrins link the ECM to the cytoskeleton and influence cell behavior (Q7).

Clinical link. Heavy smoking damages the cilia that move mucus along the airway lining (Q51).
Test bank trap. "Nine triplets in a circle" describes a basal body or centriole, not the flagellum or cilium itself (Q32).

4.8Cell-to-cell interactions

Cells make contact, "read" each other, and react.

Identity

Surface proteins give cells identity. Glycolipids are the most tissue-specific cell-surface markers. MHC proteins let the immune system recognize "self" versus "nonself" cells (Q24).

Cell connections

  • Adhesive junctions mechanically attach the cytoskeletons of neighboring cells, or attach cells to the ECM. They include adherens junctions and desmosomes; hemidesmosomes anchor cells to the matrix. In an adherens junction, cadherin anchored to actin passes through the membrane and binds the cadherin of the adjoining cell.
  • Septate or tight junctions connect the plasma membranes of adjacent cells in a sheet, so nothing leaks between them.
  • Communicating junctions let chemical or electrical signals pass directly from one cell to the next: gap junctions in animals and plasmodesmata in plants. Plasmodesmata are openings in plant cell walls that connect the cytoplasm of adjoining cells.
Clinical link. Cardiac muscle cells connect through intercalated discs containing gap junctions (electrical coupling), adherens junctions (strong, flexible links), and desmosomes (high mechanical stress) (Q49). Their contraction depends on Ca²⁺ released from the smooth ER, with signals passing through gap junctions (Q57).
